Rental property spreadsheet: how landlords track rent, expenses and profit without software

Updated 2026-10-29

Property management software starts at US$20–50 a month. With one to ten units, a well-built spreadsheet does the job for a one-time price, and you keep your data.

What to track

  • Properties and units: address, purchase price, mortgage, target rent.
  • Tenants and leases: start and end dates, deposit, monthly rent. Flag leases ending in the next 60 days.
  • Rent payments as a log (date, tenant, amount). The sheet should match payments to months and mark Paid / Partial / Overdue.
  • Expenses by category: repairs, property tax, insurance, mortgage interest, management fees, utilities. These are what your accountant asks for.
  • P&L per property: 12 months of income minus expenses, so you know which unit actually earns.

The formulas that matter

Rent status for a month: =IF(SUMIFS(Payments!C:C,Payments!B:B,$A2,Payments!D:D,E$1)>=$C2,"Paid",IF(SUMIFS(...)>0,"Partial","Overdue")). Occupancy: occupied unit-months divided by available unit-months. Leases ending soon: =AND(EndDate-TODAY()<=60, EndDate>=TODAY()).

Ready-made option

The Rental Property Manager (US$29, Excel and Google Sheets) has all of the above for up to 10 properties and 100 tenants, with a dashboard, month-by-month chart and a P&L per property. Short-term hosts should look at the Airbnb Host Tracker instead, which handles nights, occupancy, ADR and platform fees.
