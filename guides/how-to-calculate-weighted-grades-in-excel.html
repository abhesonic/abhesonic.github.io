How to calculate weighted grades in Excel and Google Sheets (with category weights, missing work and letter grades)

Updated 2026-11-24

A weighted grade counts some work more than other work – tests might be 40% of the grade and homework only 20%. A plain AVERAGE gets this wrong. Below are the formulas that get it right in Excel and Google Sheets, including the cases that trip people up: a category with no grades yet, dropped quizzes and "what do I need on the final?"

1. The basic weighted grade: SUMPRODUCT

A weighted grade is each category score multiplied by its weight, added up. If the weights add up to 100%, that sum is the grade:

Weighted grade = Score₁ × Weight₁ + Score₂ × Weight₂ + …

Example syllabus and one student's category averages:

A: CategoryB: ScoreC: Weight
2Homework92%20%
3Quizzes85%20%
4Tests78%40%
5Final exam88%20%

=SUMPRODUCT(B2:B5,C2:C5) returns 84.2% (18.4 + 17 + 31.2 + 17.6). A simple =AVERAGE(B2:B5) would say 85.75%, because it treats the homework average the same as the tests.

Enter scores and weights as percentages (type 92%, or 0.92 and format as %). If your weights do not add up to 100% – say you wrote 2, 2, 4, 2 – divide by their total: =SUMPRODUCT(B2:B5,C2:C5)/SUM(C2:C5). That version is safe either way.

2. Getting each category score from individual assignments

Most gradebooks keep one row per assignment, not one row per category. With the category in column A, points earned in B and points possible in C:

=SUMIFS(B:B,A:A,"Homework")/SUMIFS(C:C,A:A,"Homework",B:B,"<>")

The last condition, B:B,"<>", only counts the points possible for work that has a score. So three homeworks scored 9, 10 and 8 out of 10, plus a fourth that has not been graded yet, give 27 ÷ 30 = 90% instead of 27 ÷ 40 = 67.5%. That is why you leave ungraded work empty and type 0 only for work that is missing and counts as zero.

3. When a whole category has no grades yet

Early in the term there is no final exam score. SUMPRODUCT treats the empty cell as 0%, so the student's grade drops to 66.6% – which is wrong and alarming. The fix is to divide by the weights that actually have a score:

=SUMPRODUCT(B2:B5,C2:C5)/SUMPRODUCT(C2:C5*ISNUMBER(B2:B5))

With the final blank this gives 66.6 ÷ 80 = 83.25%, the student's real standing so far. Once the final is entered the bottom of the fraction becomes 100% and you get the normal grade. Check your school's policy: some teachers prefer to show the "zero for missing" version, and the two numbers answer different questions.

4. Dropping the lowest quiz

If quizzes all have the same maximum (here 10 points, scores in B2:E2 of 8, 9, 6 and 10):

=(SUM(B2:E2)-MIN(B2:E2))/((COUNT(B2:E2)-1)*10)

That is 27 ÷ 30 = 90% instead of 33 ÷ 40 = 82.5%. To drop the two lowest, subtract SMALL(B2:E2,2) as well and use COUNT(B2:E2)-2. If quizzes have different maximums, convert each to a percentage first and drop the lowest percentage, or the biggest quiz will always decide what gets dropped.

5. What score do you need on the final?

Turn the formula around. Using the table above with the final still blank:

Needed on final = (Target grade − Current weighted points) ÷ Final weight

In the sheet: =(0.85-SUMPRODUCT(B2:B4,C2:C4))/C5. For an 85% overall the student needs (85 − 66.6) ÷ 20 = 92% on the final. For 90% overall the answer is 117% – more than the maximum, so that target is out of reach. Wrap it in =IF(result>1,"Not possible",result) if students will read the sheet.

6. Turning the percentage into a letter grade

Put the cut-offs in a small table, lowest first, and look the grade up with an approximate match:

H: MinimumI: Letter
0%F
60%D
70%C
80%B
90%A

=VLOOKUP(F2,$H$2:$I$6,2,TRUE) returns B for 84.2%. In Excel 365 and Google Sheets you can also write =XLOOKUP(F2,$H$2:$H$6,$I$2:$I$6,,-1). Keeping the scale in cells beats a long nested IF: when you add plus and minus grades (B+ at 87%, say), you only add rows. Watch rounding: if your policy rounds 89.5% up to an A, look up ROUND(F2,3) with a 0.895 cut-off, or round the displayed grade the same way you report it.

7. Bonus: GPA from letter grades and credits

GPA is a weighted average too, with credits as the weights. With grade points (A = 4, B = 3, C = 2…) in B2:B5 and credits in C2:C5:

=SUMPRODUCT(B2:B5,C2:C5)/SUM(C2:C5)

Example: A in a 3-credit class, B in a 4-credit class, A in a 3-credit class and C in a 2-credit class = 40 grade points ÷ 12 credits = 3.33. Your school's scale may differ (for example A− = 3.7), so use its official table.

Common mistakes

  1. Averaging the category percentages with AVERAGE instead of weighting them.
  2. Typing 0 for work that has not been graded yet – it pulls the grade down until you fix it.
  3. Weights that add up to 90% or 110% without dividing by their sum.
  4. Mixing 92 and 92% in the same column; one is 100 times bigger than the other.
  5. Relative references in the grade-scale lookup – lock the table with $ before copying the formula down.
