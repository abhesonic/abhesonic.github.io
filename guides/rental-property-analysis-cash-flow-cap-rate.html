Rental property analysis in a spreadsheet: cash flow, cap rate, cash-on-cash and the 1% rule

Updated 2026-11-05

A rental can look fine on the listing and still lose money every month. Five numbers tell you quickly whether a deal works: NOI, cap rate, cash flow, cash-on-cash return and DSCR. Here is how to calculate each one, with a worked example you can copy into Excel or Google Sheets.

Step 1: net operating income (NOI)

NOI is the yearly rent you actually collect minus the costs of running the property. It does not include the mortgage.

  • Gross rent = monthly rent × 12
  • Effective gross income = gross rent − vacancy (5–8% is a common assumption)
  • Operating expenses = property tax + insurance + maintenance + capital expenses reserve + management + HOA + any utilities you pay
  • NOI = effective gross income − operating expenses

Step 2: the five numbers

MetricFormulaWhat it tells you
Cap rateNOI ÷ purchase priceReturn of the property itself, ignoring financing

Worked example

A $200,000 house rents for $1,800 a month. You put 25% down ($50,000) plus $6,000 closing costs and borrow $150,000 at 7% for 30 years.

  • Gross rent $21,600; 5% vacancy → effective income $20,520
  • Expenses: tax $2,400, insurance $1,200, maintenance 8% ($1,728), capex reserve 5% ($1,080), management 8% ($1,728) = $8,136
  • NOI = $12,384 → cap rate 6.2%
  • Mortgage: =PMT(7%/12,360,-150000) = $998 a month, $11,975 a year
  • Cash flow = $12,384 − $11,975 = $409 a year (about $34 a month)
  • Cash-on-cash = $409 ÷ $56,000 = 0.7%; DSCR = 1.03; break-even occupancy ≈ 93%

A 6% cap rate sounds acceptable, but at 7% interest the financing eats almost everything. One bad repair and the year is negative. This is exactly what the spreadsheet is for: change the price, rent or down payment and watch whether the deal survives.

Quick screens before the full analysis

  • 1% rule: monthly rent should be at least 1% of the price. Here $1,800 < $2,000, so it fails – a warning sign, not a verdict.
  • 50% rule: operating expenses tend to be about half of rent over time. If your estimate is far below 50%, check what you left out.
  • Gross rent multiplier: price ÷ yearly gross rent (here 9.3). Lower is better; compare it with other listings in the same area.

If you flip instead of rent: the 70% rule

For a fix-and-flip, the maximum you should offer is after-repair value × 70% − repair costs. A house worth $300,000 after a $45,000 rehab supports an offer of at most $300,000 × 0.7 − $45,000 = $165,000. The remaining 30% covers closing, holding, selling costs and your profit.
