How to calculate food cost percentage (with Excel and Google Sheets formulas)

Updated 2026-11-17

Food cost percentage tells you how much of every dollar a guest pays goes back out on ingredients. There are two versions – one for the whole kitchen over a month and one for a single plate – and you need both. Here are the formulas, a worked example for each, and the spreadsheet layout that keeps them up to date.

General information for menu costing, not financial or accounting advice.

1. Food cost % for a period (the whole kitchen)

This is the number your accountant or P&L shows. It uses inventory counts, so it catches waste, theft, over-portioning and spoilage as well as what was actually sold.

Food cost % = (Beginning inventory + Purchases − Ending inventory) ÷ Food sales

The part in brackets is the cost of food used (cost of goods sold). Example for one month:

ItemAmount
Beginning inventory (count on the 1st)$8,400
Food purchases during the month$21,600
Ending inventory (count on the last day)$7,900
Cost of food used$22,100
Food sales$72,000
Food cost %30.7%

In a sheet with those values in B2:B5 (beginning, purchases, ending, sales): =(B2+B3-B4)/B5, formatted as a percentage. Two rules keep it honest: count inventory on the same day and at the same time every period, and use food sales only (keep drinks and their cost in a separate beverage cost %).

2. Food cost % for one dish (plate cost)

The period number tells you that something is wrong; the plate cost tells you which dish. For each dish you add up what every ingredient in one portion costs and divide by the menu price:

Plate food cost % = Plate cost ÷ Selling price

The trap is ingredient cost. You pay for the whole fish or the whole sack of potatoes, but trimming, peeling and cooking lose part of it. That is the yield %, and it belongs in the formula:

Cost of an ingredient in the dish = Pack price ÷ (Pack size × Yield %) × Quantity used

Worked example – a grilled salmon plate selling for $26:

IngredientPackPack priceYieldUsedCost
Salmon fillet2,000 g$46.0085%180 g$4.87
Potatoes10,000 g$12.5080%200 g$0.31
Asparagus1,000 g$8.8070%100 g$1.26
Butter454 g$4.50100%15 g$0.15
Lemon1 each$0.40100%¼$0.10
Garnish, oil, saltfixed allowance$0.15
Plate cost$6.84

Food cost % = 6.84 ÷ 26 = 26.3%, and the plate leaves $19.16 towards labour, rent and profit. Without the yield column the salmon would cost $4.14 instead of $4.87 and the dish would look cheaper than it is.

Spreadsheet layout: one row per recipe line with pack price in C, pack size in D, yield in E and quantity used in F. The line cost is =C2/(D2*E2)*F2; the plate cost is =SUM(G2:G7) plus the garnish allowance. Keep pack prices on a separate Ingredients sheet and look them up (XLOOKUP in Excel 365 and Google Sheets, or INDEX/MATCH in older Excel), so a price change from your supplier updates every dish at once.

3. The price that hits your target food cost

Turn the formula around: Menu price = Plate cost ÷ Target food cost %. For the salmon at a 30% target: 6.84 ÷ 0.30 = $22.80. Round it to a menu-friendly step with =CEILING(G8/0.3,0.5), which gives $23.00 (29.7% food cost). Many full-service restaurants land somewhere around the high 20s to mid 30s percent, but the right target depends on your concept, so use your own numbers rather than an industry average.

4. Why the lowest food cost % is not always the best dish

You pay rent with dollars, not percentages. Compare contribution margin (price − plate cost) as well:

DishPricePlate costFood cost %Margin per plate
Chicken Pasta$18$3.6020.0%$14.40
Ribeye Steak$34$13.6040.0%$20.40

The steak has double the food cost % but earns $6 more on every plate. Cutting it because "40% is too high" could lower your profit.

5. Put cost and popularity together: the menu-engineering matrix

The classic method (Kasavana & Smith) sorts each dish by two questions: is it popular, and is its margin above average? Using one month of sales for five mains (960 plates in total):

DishUnits soldMenu mixMarginClass
Classic Burger42043.8%$11.20Plowhorse
Chicken Pasta26027.1%$14.40Star
Grilled Salmon15015.6%$19.16Star
Ribeye Steak707.3%$20.40Puzzle
Caesar Salad606.2%$10.10Dog
  • Popular means a menu mix of at least 70% of a fair share: 0.7 × (1 ÷ 5 dishes) = 14%. With units in D2:D6: =IF(D2/SUM($D$2:$D$6)>=0.7/COUNT($D$2:$D$6),"High","Low")
  • High margin means at or above the weighted average margin, here $13,356 ÷ 960 = $13.91. With margins in E2:E6: =IF(E2>=SUMPRODUCT($E$2:$E$6,$D$2:$D$6)/SUM($D$2:$D$6),"High","Low")
  • Class: =IF(G2="High",IF(H2="High","Star","Plowhorse"),IF(H2="High","Puzzle","Dog"))

What to do with each: keep and feature Stars; nudge the price of Plowhorses up or trim their plate cost (the burger sells well but earns the least per plate); promote, rename or reposition Puzzles; rework or remove Dogs. Compare dishes within the same category – starters against starters – or the averages get skewed.

Common mistakes

  1. Using last year's ingredient prices. Update pack prices whenever an invoice changes.
  2. Forgetting yield, sauces, oil and garnish. Small items add up across hundreds of plates.
  3. Comparing plate cost % with the period food cost %. A gap between them is useful: it points to waste or portions that are bigger than the recipe.
  4. Judging dishes only by percentage instead of margin and sales.
